00 — Context

The product didn't fail. The world changed — twice.

Most startup failures are internal — wrong team, wrong execution, wrong market. Dror's story is different. We built the right product twice. Each time, an external event made our market disappear. Understanding that distinction is what this case study is really about.

Forcing function #1 · March 2020

COVID-19 locks everyone home

We were 6 months into building a citizen safety app — location sharing, SOS alerts, safety circles for people moving through cities. Like Life360, but for India.

Citizens stop moving. Our core use case — safety while in transit — becomes irrelevant indefinitely. B2C growth stalls. Revenue stays near zero. We have to find a new problem or die.

Forcing function #2 · Late 2021

COVID gets controlled. Restrictions lift.

We had pivoted to B2B workplace safety. Enterprises were paying for Bluetooth-based social distancing tools for essential workers. Revenue was real. Contracts were signed.

The urgency disappears. Offices reopen fully. Clients stop renewing. The problem we solved no longer exists at the severity that made people pay. Company winds down.

Role reality

There was no time to be a PM in the traditional sense

In a 10-person team under survival pressure, I couldn't be ahead of the team thinking three sprints out. I collapsed the PM → Design → Dev handoff into a single person across three time blocks per day. The product moved because I refused to be a bottleneck.

Act 1 May – Oct 2020 · 6 months

Building India's Life360 — from scratch

The CEO had an early MVP and initial seed funding. My job was to take that signal and build a real product. Life360 existed but wasn't built for India — it assumed reliable connectivity, English literacy, and smartphone penetration that tier 2/3 India didn't have in 2020.

The market gap — why Life360 didn't work for India

Gap 01

Connectivity assumption

Life360 required persistent internet. In tier 2/3 India, patchy connectivity made real-time location sharing unreliable exactly when it mattered.

Gap 02

English-first UI

Life360's onboarding was English-heavy and jargon-dense. Our primary users in smaller cities needed icon-first, low-literacy design.

Gap 03

Family tracking ≠ safety in India

The "track your family" framing felt invasive in Indian social dynamics. We repositioned as a safety circle — opt-in, mutual, trust-first.

Gap 04 · Research finding

Usability test: 68% drop-off in Life360 onboarding

We ran Life360 with 15 Indian users. Primary drop-off reasons: confusing permissions flow, English UI, and assumption that all members have active smartphones.

What we built — the consumer app

Three decisions that defined V1:

Decision 01 · MVP scope

Full safety suite or single reliable action?

✓ Chose

Single SOS trigger + safety circle setup. One core action, bulletproof before anything else shipped.

✗ Rejected

Full feature suite — live tracking, community reporting, in-app emergency call, incident logging.

Why: In a trust-sensitive category, one failure destroys retention permanently. We shipped less and made it reliable.

Decision 02 · Confirmation dialog

Should one tap send SOS, or should we add a confirmation step?

✓ Chose

No confirmation. One tap = alert sent. Accepted false positives for speed under genuine stress.

✗ Rejected

Two-step: tap to arm, tap to confirm. Safer in calm conditions, dangerous when it actually mattered.

Why: Usability testing showed 3× longer completion time under simulated stress with confirmation. Those seconds aren't recoverable.

The commercial reality of Act 1

The consumer app launched. We got real users. But the revenue model was broken from the start — B2C freemium in India in 2020 meant most users never paid. The product was validated socially, not commercially. Then COVID hit and citizens stopped moving. Our core use case — safety while in transit — became irrelevant. We had a live product, real users, and almost no revenue. Something had to change.

The Pivot Oct – Nov 2020 · 6 weeks

The decision that changed everything

Dhiraj was in conversations with enterprises about employee safety. The signal was clear: companies with essential workers in factories and offices needed exactly what we'd built — but packaged for B2B, with Bluetooth proximity detection and a management dashboard instead of a consumer SOS button. He brought it to me. We validated fast.

The pivot decision · Oct 2020

Keep building the consumer app, or rebuild for B2B?

✓ Chose — Pivot to B2B

Rebuild for enterprise. New users: factory managers and essential workers. New model: monthly B2B contracts. New tech: Bluetooth proximity + smart card hardware. Keep consumer app live but stop investing in it.

✗ Rejected — Stay B2C

Wait for COVID to end. Continue iterating on the consumer app. Hope the market comes back. No revenue path for 12–18 months minimum.

What made this hard: We had spent 6 months building the consumer product. Pivoting meant accepting that our original thesis — India's Life360 — was commercially unviable in the current environment. It also meant rebuilding with the same team, in the same timeline, with no additional resources. The prototype had to be good enough to close clients before we'd committed to the full rebuild.

How the product worked

Layer 01 · Hardware

Smart cards for every worker

Each essential worker carried a Bluetooth-enabled smart card. Cards detected proximity to other cards. When two workers got too close for too long, both devices vibrated and logged the event.

Layer 02 · Mobile

Phone-based detection for managers

Workers with smartphones used the mobile app as a secondary detection layer. This reduced hardware cost for enterprises where some workers already had devices.

Layer 03 · Dashboard

Real-time compliance view for managers

Factory managers got a web dashboard showing: active worker count, proximity events, compliance score, at-risk zones. This is what I designed nights and coded afternoons in React.

Layer 04 · Reporting

Exportable reports for enterprise compliance

Enterprises needed documentation for regulatory compliance. Weekly PDF reports with distancing metrics, event logs, and trend lines. A feature I added after the first client asked.

Honest assessment — what the revenue story actually means

₹1.98Cr sounds like a success. In context it isn't. The company raised $494K (~₹4Cr) and generated ₹1.98Cr in lifetime revenue. The B2B pivot worked commercially — but only for as long as COVID made social distancing a compliance requirement. Once restrictions lifted, the problem we'd built for stopped being urgent enough for enterprises to pay for. We proved we could sell. We didn't prove the market would last.

Reflection

"We didn't fail because we built the wrong product. We built the right product for a temporary world. The lesson isn't 'don't pivot.' It's 'understand what your market is made of — and whether it exists without the forcing function that created it.'"

The correlation that ended us

Our revenue tracked COVID severity — not product quality. When restrictions lifted, revenue fell. That's not a sustainable business.

Quarterly revenue (₹L) COVID restriction intensity (proxy)
Revenue and COVID restriction intensity track together through 2021, then both decline in 2022.

What I'd do differently

Build for the post-COVID use case in parallel. Workplace safety as a category doesn't require a pandemic — but we never found the non-emergency version of our product. If we'd started that search in early 2021, we might have had something before the urgency disappeared.

The product-market fit lesson

PMF tied to an external forcing function is not durable PMF. Our retention was high, our NPS was strong, clients were happy. But none of that mattered when the underlying reason to buy disappeared. True PMF survives when the conditions that created it change.

What this changed in how I work

I now ask "what happens to this product when the forcing function goes away?" before committing to any product direction. It's the question we never asked at Dror — because the forcing function felt permanent at the time.

The operational learning

Designing nights, writing PRDs at noon, coding afternoons — that rhythm worked because I refused to be a bottleneck. But it's not scalable. The real lesson: in a lean team, the PM has to be willing to do whatever the product needs, not just what's in their job description.
